01

Prof. Antje Blumenthal

Frazer Institute

Session 1 · 9:00 AM

Biography

Antje Blumenthal leads the INFECTION THEME and heads the Infection & Inflammation Group at The University of Queensland Frazer Institute. She is an ARC Future Fellow and Director of the Australian Infectious Diseases Research Centre, an interactive research network connecting academia, industry, and health services. Leveraging her expertise in microbiology and immunology, her research aims to improve treatment options for severe bacterial infections, with a specific focus on tuberculosis and sepsis. The Blumenthal lab applies cutting-edge technologies in model systems and patient samples, and fosters cross-disciplinary collaborations across academia, clinical teams and industry partners.

Keynote talk

Talk title

Molecular regulation of inflammation and antimicrobial defence during bacterial infection.

Abstract

Host defence mechanisms against bacterial infections are essential for survival, but if unchecked can cause acute tissue injury and chronic disease. This presentation explores molecular mechanisms that initiate, amplify, and regulate inflammatory and antimicrobial responses to bacterial infection. By integrating findings from preclinical models and patient-derived samples, this research aims to advance fundamental understanding of how host defence against bacterial infections is initiated and switched off, with the ultimate goal of identifying therapeutic targets for host-directed interventions to combat bacterial pathogens regardless of their antibiotic susceptibility profile.

02

Prof. Steven Djordjevic

University of Technology Sydney (UTS)

Session 2 · 11:15 AM

Biography

Steven Djordjevic is a Distinguished Professor of Infectious Diseases and Theme Lead for Microbial Genomics & Proteomics at the Australian Institute for Microbiology & Infectious Diseases at the University of Technology, Sydney in Australia. The overarching goal of his team’s research is to understand the evolutionary drivers of antimicrobial resistance and microbial pathogenesis through a One Health lens by applying advanced genomic and molecular approaches to shed light on how bacterial pathogen evolve, spread and acquire resistance to antibiotics, with important implications for human health, veterinary medicine and environmental health. He is a co-founder of the Australian Centre for Genomic Epidemiological Microbiology, a collaborative initiative advancing the application of genomic technologies to infectious disease surveillance and research with a focus on Escherichia coli and other Gram negative ESKAPEE pathogens. His team undertakes microbial genomics as it pertains to phylogeny and epidemiology, plasmid evolution, and pathogen emergence as well as the identification and characterization of mobile elements that carry genes for AMR, virulence, resistance to chlorine-based oxidizing agents and heat- all of which impact human health and safe food production. He has extensive collaborations across Australia and abroad where he: i) co-leads the One Health program in Australian Pathogen Genomics Program (AusPathogens 2020-2025; 2026-2029) with the Doherty Institute; ii) plays a lead role in coordinating UTS’s participation as a Tier 1 partner in CRC-SAAFE, Australia’s Cooperative Research Centre for Solving Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) in Agribusiness, Food and Environments (2023-2033); iii) undertakes microbial genomics-based, One Health surveillance efforts in Fiji and Samoa and iv) is the Australian lead in an international project funded by The Joint Programming Initiative on Antimicrobial Resistance (JPIAMR) involving researchers in Israel, Germany, United Kingdom, South Africa and Canada.

Keynote talk

Talk title

The demise of traditional Escherichia coli pathotypes and the emergence of hybrid pathotype lineages: A One Health perspective

Abstract

E. coli is the leading cause (75-90%) of urinary tract (UTI) and blood stream infections (BSI) and a major cause of ventilatory-associated pneumonia in critical care wards, neonatal meningitis, and intra-abdominal-, wound- and surgical site infections, ranking it as the most common cause of Gram-negative infections in humans. Invasive E. coli disease is on an upward trajectory globally, driven by a combination of aging populations, rising comorbidity rates, and escalating antimicrobial resistance (AMR). E. coli increasingly displays resistance to multiple antibiotic classes (MDR) with ~ 65% of invasive human infections showing resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (3GCs) in many regions. Modelling studies suggest that 3GCs and carbapenems may be compromised for a sizeable proportion of E. coli by 2030. Garnering a fuller appreciation of potential reservoirs for ExPEC remains challenging as does a deeper understanding of the roles of wildlife and insects in transmission of MDR E. coli. Metals, biocides, antibiotic breakdown products and some pharmaceutical agents are confirmed/suspected co-selective agents for the persistence and evolution of AMR E. coli. While understanding drivers of AMR and co-selection are important, so too is an appreciation of mechanisms that underpin resistance to heat and chlorine-based oxidizing agents, critical for water sanitation and food safety. Of great concern are increasing reports of the isolation of hybrid lineages of E. coli from humans and food animals, particularly those that carry ExPEC virulence gene cargo. An appreciation of these concepts underpins the development of economically sustainable One Health genomic surveillance programs in Australia and our experiences in this regard will be discussed.

03

Prof. Gregory Cook

University of Otago

Session 3 · 1:45 PM

Biography

Greg Cook is a global leader in microbial metabolism and energetics, turning discovery into new weapons against antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Using Mycobacterium tuberculosis as a model, he pioneered drugs that disrupt respiration and ATP synthesis. An inaugural Sesquicentennial Distinguished Professor, he led Otago’s Department of Microbiology and Immunology and held senior roles at Queensland University of Technology. He has published more than 300 papers, mentored over 100 postgraduate students, and led major national and international programes. A Fellow of the Royal Society of New Zealand and recipient of Otago’s Distinguished Research Medal, he has attracted substantial industry investment to advance new technologies for combatting AMR. Greg is currently the Deputy Vice-Chancellor, Research & Innovation at the University of Otago.

Keynote talk

Talk title

Exploiting Hidden Redox and Metabolic Vulnerabilities in Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis

Abstract

Drug-resistant tuberculosis remains a major global health challenge; however, resistance mutations can disrupt essential cellular processes and expose previously unrecognised bacterial vulnerabilities. We combined genetic screening, phenotypic growth assays, and metabolomic profiling to determine how drug resistance remodels the physiology and metabolism of Mycobacterium tuberculosis under host-relevant conditions. The fitness costs associated with resistance varied according to the available carbon source, while genetically unrelated resistance mutations produced convergent phenotypes and metabolic dysregulation affecting nutrient utilisation, energy generation, and protein synthesis. Bedaquiline- and rifampicin-resistant mutants exhibited impaired growth on host-relevant carbon sources and condition-dependent collateral susceptibility to antibiotics. Rifampicin-resistant mutants were particularly vulnerable to perturbations in sulphur metabolism and redox homeostasis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that drug resistance constrains the metabolic adaptation of M. tuberculosis within the host. Therapeutically exploiting these liabilities may enable the development of more effective treatment strategies and regimens for drug-resistant tuberculosis.
